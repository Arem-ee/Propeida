# The Lekki Headmaster — the complete revision

You're sitting Section A of Use of English, and ten questions come from one book. This sheet is the whole novel in our own words, with nothing close to the actual text — read it twice and Section A holds no surprises.

Kabir Alabi Garba's The Lekki Headmaster was published in 2023 by Winepress (2024 copyright; the BASMALLAH COMMS edition; ISBN 978-978-60211-2-6). It tells the story of **Adebepo Adewale, called Bepo** — the long-serving principal of Stardom Schools in Lekki, Lagos, who climbs slowly from doubting his own exit to choosing his school over moving abroad. This sheet is written to stand alone, covering plot, names, numbers, places, processes, themes and the traps JAMB sets. Learn it properly and you need no other summary. Read the book itself when you can, but you can sit Section A without a single blank.

**The shape of the whole story** — Stardom is an elite private school in Lekki whose routine runs on assembly, examinations, staff meetings, excursions, cultural programmes and discipline. Bepo's wife Seri, a nurse in London, pushes him to move to the UK, along with their children Nike and Kike, who are already settled there. He loves his learners and is torn. The twelve chapters run Dusk to Dawn as a day-night circle — the breakdown at the start, the passport pains in the middle, the farewell, the flight, and the return.

## Chapter 1 — Dusk (PDF 4–8; printed pages 5–9)

A normal morning assembly turns chaotic. Bepo walks to the podium from the back of the school, moving slowly, smiling little, and takes the microphone from **Angel, the chapel prefect** — at the exact moment the *pep talk*, which started seven years earlier, has everyone excited. The speaker is **Ikenna Egbu**, an SSS science poet from a broadcasting family, who is back from an excursion to Jos in Plateau State. He describes Lamingo Dam, the University of Jos, the Shere Hills, Wase Rocks and the Solomon Lar **Amusement Park**, and praises Jos over London or New York. He closes by saying he would rather settle in Jos than *japa* to Canada or the UK. His mother is an award-winning poet, a broadcaster, and Ikenna mixes good results with verse.

Then Bepo freezes. He tries to speak and nothing comes out. His head drops to his shoulders, his eyes drop to the floor tiles, and he cries — first in trickles, then in a full breakdown — and drops the microphone, which clatters off the twin speakers. **Vice-Principal Grace Apeh** and four staff rush to him. **Mrs Titi, the nurse**, brings a handkerchief. **Mr Justus Anabel, the chemistry teacher**, is told to stop the assembly. Apeh, the nurse and **Mr Oyelana, the CRK teacher**, walk him to his office with his face buried in his hands. He lies there softly for over 30 minutes while more teachers arrive. The **guidance counsellor, Mrs Beke Egbin**, joins the small group, and then the group moves into **the MD's office on the second floor of the Admin Building** — the proprietor **Mrs Ibidun Gloss**, “popularly called MD”, who drives in from her morning business, takes off her glasses, drops them on a tea stool, and receives him. He still will not say why. The MD calls in **Mr Oye Wande, the physics teacher and pastor**; he sits beside Bepo, reminds him of his important position, and hears nothing. Bepo writes his wife's international number on the back of a card he takes from his grey suit pocket. Two calls do not ring. She sends him home, thinking the school is a place for learning and earning, not a rehabilitation centre — and that parents will keep pressing her.

Context JAMB can use:

- **Boarding fees were cut** from ₦250,000 to ₦165,000 per session, and above 80% of parents moved their children into boarding. Punctuality jumped — almost everyone was at assembly by 7:45 a.m. The school then quietly raised **Excursion and Other Items** by ₦93,000. **Mr Audu, the fine-arts teacher** — the staffroom's sharpest wit — jokes that the MD is "a witch and a wizard rolled into one".
- The assembly runs the **second stanza of the National Anthem as prayer on Tuesdays and Thursdays** ("Oh God of creation, direct our noble cause…") and Christian/Muslim prayers plus the first stanza on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, before the school anthem.
- The previous day, Stardom had celebrated **above 90% WASSCE success** with snacks, wine and ₦20,000 to all SSS 3 teachers, plus ₦30,000 to teachers of distinction-scoring subjects. Only two teachers had two-candidates-with-Ds lines to answer to — **Mr Obong Ukaku (Chemistry) and Miss Taye Kareem (Geography)** — but no candidate scored F9, so nobody was dismissed (Stardom's rule is that an F9 gets the teacher sacked; the previous year even the MD's cousin **Mr Funso Daniels** was not spared).
- The staff tease Bepo that he answers every quarrel with the same calm manner, the way the old TV drama Village Headmaster's **King Oloja** resolved matters. He mimics the character a bit too well, and the nickname sticks — **"The Lekki Headmaster"** — even after he becomes principal. He also always corrects the pronunciation of "Principo": **it is "PrinciPL", not "Principo".**

## Chapter 2 — The Enticement (PDF 9–12; printed pages 10–13)

After about five days of what feels like interrogation, Bepo admits it: he plans to **leave Nigeria for the United Kingdom**. His marriage was at the brink because he kept dragging his feet. Seri, the nurse, is in London with **Nike** (now at university) and **Kike** (still at secondary school), and the teaching post in the UK is secured. He loves Stardom and his learners, and he feels it is wrong to abandon a developing nation for an already-developed one — but family pressure is stronger.

The background economics, drawn so you can use them:

- Stardom pays Bepo about **₦400,000 a month**. The UK post promises about **£3,600 a month** in entitlements. At about **₦1,700 to the pound**, that prospective income reads as about **₦6 million a month**. **Mr Oyelana, the CRK teacher**, points out that Bepo will spend in pounds, not naira, which weakens the money-sent-home argument; **Audu** waves that away — if Bepo sends £1,000 home a month, that is above ₦1.7 million, and nobody making ₦400,000 can match it. Audu mocks him as "bewitched", pushes books under his armpit, announces he is "heading to the airport" — and is late for class, as usual. His jokes save him.
- Bepo had been planning to **retire at 55** — four years away. He had been attending entrepreneurship seminars, picturing himself founding **a school of his own** after Stardom (his experience plus endless ambition) or, failing that, **transport leasing**, and smaller ventures: a farm, food, sachet pure water. The cautionary tale of his own failure is always in his mouth — **Fruitful Future School**, the small neighbourhood school he and a colleague started shortly after National Youth Service, which **collapsed because of socio-economic challenges in the location**. Seri would never finance any project that tied him to Nigeria.

Something worse happened earlier in his life. Years before, Bepo was pinched out of a previous job and went without any salary. At "Tyana Ipaja" (as the OCR pages 27–28 read — a crowded, Agbeni-style face-me-I-face-you house in Iyana Ipaja, Lagos), at the height of his financial drought, he **"ate" ₦2,500 of tenants' electricity tariff money he had collected** and lied about paying it over. When NEPA eventually cut the cable, the angry tenants discovered the truth, mocked him as "Oga Tisa" (Teacher Oga), and **Iya Mathew**, the fierce wife of his co-tenant Mr Adio, leapt onto him with a bowl of **elubo (cassava powder)** dumped full over his head while singing "Oga Tisa, ole! O na owo ina!" — "Mr Teacher, thief! He spent the electricity tariff!" That shame is the cruelest moment Bepo carries. It explains his intolerance for waiting around jobless.
## Chapter 3 — Migration Tales (PDF 13–17; printed pages 14–18)

Once Bepo is leaning toward London, he floods himself with stories from the diaspora — and this chapter is where JAMB plucks its most repeated facts. The main claims, told in Bepo's voice as he listens to tales from abroad:

- **Hourly pay works abroad.** A friend in the US says you can earn **between $150 and $250 a day**, up to four days a week — about **$2,400 a month** — usually the way people chase their japa dreams. The Yoruba proverb at issue is *"oga ta, oga o ta, owo alaaru a pe"* — whether an employer gains or loses, the worker still takes home his full pay. That is called the *pragmatic wage* system, and it is held up as one of the ideals powering developed countries.
- People ignore the down-side: rent, taxes and maintenance are paid in hard currencies. What remains still transfers as good sums home. The Nigerian who switched from driving to a maintenance job earning **$500 a day** works out to about **₦800,000 daily** — and even toilet-cleaners are out-earned by cabbies abroad.
- **The government admits about 3,000 doctors emigrate yearly** — mass emigration includes many graduates and nurses, especially those **pursuing Master's degrees**. Study visas allow the holder, spouse and children, so families sell *all* their assets (houses, land, cars, office and home goods). Some sold what wasn't theirs; some fled before loan repayment; some repay slowly from abroad; some never look back. Stardom gossip from Bepo's own administration adds to the pile: **Mr Nku**, who took a ₦2 million cooperative loan and vanished in two days into the "japa wind"; and **a school driver** who tried to steal a school bus to fund his son's college education abroad and confessed to the police.
- **Sola** — former Home Economics teacher at Stardom, sacked quietly into a three-month suspension for setting up a crèche near the school (Bepo led the investigation which showed it was casual labour, and saved her from sacking, so she kept her pay after monitoring) — emigrated to the UK six months before, from London with her husband, and now works the British system and phones to brief Bepo. Renting instead of paying fees — they sold everything and still borrowed about **₦4 million** — she is currently supporting her husband's Master's from her earnings. She earns up to **£200 a day**. Manchester is their base because London rents of £1,000–£1,500 are far beyond their means; the flat there is not furnished and costs **£650 a month**, paid month by month, never a year ahead; the rail from Manchester to London takes **about 30 minutes**; children there get **free primary and secondary schooling** (Nike and Kike were also beneficiaries of UK free schooling before Nike began to pay university fees, and Sola's wife paid the bulk); there are education loans and scholarships for poor homes; a three-year-old who had a seizure drew **two ambulances plus a car in under five minutes**, had her birthday in hospital, and got gifts showered on her by the staff.
- **Jare** is a young man who left a banking job and wept to find that caring for an old couple in London had docked his dignity. **Hope** is an accountant who applied for a UK Master's supported by his wife; barely four months after landing, she simply stopped paying, saying "the demands of her job are too heavy" — and her parents in Nigeria agreed with her verdict.
- **Riike**, a woman now in the US, in under three years bought **two houses in Ibadan**; she has three children in fee-free primary and secondary foundation schools (till 2027, when the first will reach university); she has brought two sisters across, and hopes to bring her brother **Wemimo** — the youngest — once visa hurdles are cleared.
- **Akindele** is a man who moved to the US (invited by a US citizen he met at a Lagos media company) at 55, after an eighteen-year marriage ended in divorce — three children between 16 and 10. He now lives with his new woman. His first job was freight loading, followed by **correctional service**; he notes he cannot invite any family yet because he needs time and financial base. He says flat out: "there is no easy job in America" — even Nigerian retired permanent secretaries end up as casual workers moving mail at 70.

## Chapter 4 — A Case of Visa Denied (PDF 18–22; printed pages 19–23)

**Mrs Ignatius**, a parent at Stardom, calls Bepo at his room at **Adeniyi Jones, Ikeja**, at **11:55pm**; she sobs that her son **Ibe** is not home yet. She is one of those parents who stretch his cordiality by discussing their marital life. During the call Bepo learns the full story:

- Her oil-firm-manager husband, Ibe, had bought into japa — they had been planning to relocate to London. He complained he was sick of the system and vowed no child would be left behind. The economic maths drew them: **$1,000 remittance a month at ₦1,600 black-market rate is about ₦1.6 million** — an amount even a senior lawyer's salary (₦15,000 a month, as the narration has it) cannot beat.
- Mrs Ignatius had learned tailoring and hairdressing, including **irun didi** and the bridal art. She had pulled a start as a clerical officer and gone for specific **Yoruba braids** (suku, kojusoko, onilegogoro), with the promise of **$100 per head** in the UK — about ₦160,000 a client. Her husband had wound down his clearing-and-forwarding side business to prepare.
- At the advanced visa stage, an **DNA test showed Ibe is not the father of their eldest daughter, Favour — 15, an SS2 girl at Stardom** — and the family never survived the fallout. The visa was refused. The wife tells Bepo that Ibe refuses to believe it; he says "Favour and I have to go". Favour does not know the saga; her brother **Lyi** suspects why the visas were refused, because visa rules require paternity evidence for applicants below 18. Ibe left home two hours before Bepo finishes the call, and Bepo thinks immediately of a *memo* on school events as a possible book — a recurring motif in his head.

A second thread runs through the same chapter. **Mrs Mary Ladele** watches a Nollywood romance film at home at 1 p.m. in GRA, with her husband Dele — a senior civil servant in Abuja — sleeping children, and a mild rule that bed is at 10 p.m. Her enemy is **Mr Ayesoro**, a Government teacher with deep tribal marks whom pupils nickname **Mr Wala / Mr Owata** for his cuts. He meets three-month-old **Bibi** at a Saturday basketball practice; the child is terrified; she dreams of him repeatedly; her relations eventually force a house transfer to **Green House**; the Stardom management, anxious about losing the Ladele kids to a rival school, **transfers Ayesoro to Stardom Hub, the property arm of the Stardom Group of Companies** — he is diverted from teaching altogether.

## Chapter 5 — Snake in the Roof (PDF 23–25; printed pages 24–26)

Behind the drama, a corridor scene: lunch break at Stardom, about 1,500 students and staff. The MD has a strange, private illness — chronic sitting pain linked to a back condition. She rests in a private room inside her spacious office for about ten minutes several times a day, unknown to most of the community, after doctors within and outside the country — and even spiritual and traditional treatments — have failed. One afternoon she walks off on a sudden impulse to inspect the land behind the school, acquired two years earlier, about five minutes from the back gate. At the gate, a guard — **Jombo** — tells her the parked cars belong to staff. She sees that about half of the three plots are packed with staff vehicles — Toyota (the "Muscle" ones included), Hyundai, Venza, Mazda — and two buses far at the back, one white, one the ubiquitous Lagos yellow. She suspects embezzlement and summons the principal and the school accountant, **Jeremi Amos**. On arrival he begins his soft, soothing talk; she interrupts him: "Accountant, I didn't call you for a motivational speech." The two explain that most of the cars are funded through a **school cooperative** — the purse holds **₦95 million**, of which over **₦50 million** had been loaned out, and deductions are taken straight from source. Nobody is stealing.

Her mother — **Chief Mrs Solape Bayo**, chair of a family board that includes Martins Bayo (non-executive director) and Oye Bayo (the last-born, the board secretary), a board that convenes the next day — calls the family board. Their verdict: **no staff member may borrow more than ₦250,000**; every loan requires **the MD's sign-off**; and **the management must be duly informed of cooperative elections**. Her remark is the famous one: "It's like hanging a snake in the roof and going to bed."
## Chapter 6 — Ade as Well as Jide Comes vs. Come (PDF 26–32; printed pages 27–33)

**Open Day** is one of the biggest deals at Stardom — parents attend, staff must be at their best, and some have been fired for dress-code violations. The best-dressed teacher is identified and prized at session's end. Open Days are two-edged: parents come bearing gifts (the **Gbayi family** — Mrs Nike Gbayi, wife of an employee of a leading beverage company, sends the school packets of beverages as gifts) and also come loaded with complaints. Any teacher who steps on a pupil's toes gets branded and punished.

Into the hornet's nest walks a case that nearly costs a teacher his job. **Mr Fafore** — an English teacher and single parent of two, with 22 years of experience — commutes from his **self-built, unplastered house in Ifo, Ogun State**; his salary has stood at **₦175,000**; he cannot afford the Lekki two-bedroom rents (over ₦2 million a year), which is why he wakes at **4 a.m.**, leaves home at **4:30 a.m.**, reaches Stardom by **6 a.m.**, and naps at his desk for about an hour before colleagues arrive. He has **twice won the Most Punctual Teacher Award**. His family side: two children, a third on the way, and school fees owed. The trigger is **Mr Guta** — father of Dorah (SS2) and Nicholas (JSS2) — who spots what looks like an error in the exercise books Fafore marked, **"Ade as well as Jide comes early,"** and the MD, in the heat of an emergency meeting, threatens to dismiss him publicly in front of the whole staff.

Bepo intervenes calmly. In standard English, verbs in phrases such as *as well as*, *together with* and *alongside* are **singular governed** — the verb agrees with "Ade", not with the plural "Jide" — so it is "comes", not "come". The MD orders every member of staff to check online; every one of them confirms that Fafore and the principal are correct; the MD stumbles, humiliated. **Audu** dissolves the tension with mock-courtroom English: "Fafore as well as the Principal is hereby pardoned, discharged and acquitted. And the MD is also pardoned." The room roars. The storm passes.

Also embedded here, from years back: a pretty pupil whose handwriting the teacher made write to the board for others to copy was the basis of a **lawsuit** filed by the accountant-oil-firm father of the girl. The school paid out of court, the girl received one tuition-free term, and the teacher received a three-month no-pay suspension. That is why Stardom stopped note-copying — and why the photogenic girl's lesson-ghost still haunts teachers.

## Chapter 7 — Ritualists (PDF 33–39; printed pages 34–40)

Stardom's secrets echo through an earlier school Bepo taught at: **Beesway Group of School**, in Ojikutu, on the outskirts of Lagos, where he served as senior English teacher under its director, **Mr Egi Meko**. Bepo repeatedly corrected the school's grammatical name — "Group of School" should read **"Group of Schools"**, because a group must contain more than one school. Meko refused — he invoked divine inspiration, CAC registration, and the Ministry of Education ("the name was divinely inspired… it has a spiritual undertone; you are not a kid"). PTA meetings became battlefields: a parent raised the point and was backed by others; Meko forced Bepo to eat his tongue in the meeting, calling him "too-know". The Yoruba proverb invoked was *"the vegetable seller who retorts 'these are not dumpsite vegetables'"* — as if Bepo were the guilty one protesting too much.

Two nights after, at about **2:30 a.m.**, the floodlights fail while Bepo lies awake in the staff quarters. Two hundred metres away, the houses are still lit. He investigates — five men, a cow and a pit. The director is there, directing the burial of a cow alive in a pit — an occult ritual to increase enrolment or yield something. Bepo strides in shouting; a man clubs him on the wrist with a log; two others beat him; he is forced to the ground. The director orders "handle him gently"; his men comply; Bepo recovers and says nothing. Later he discovers exactly what happens to a parent who tries the same trick — he asks **Mr Ogo**, a parent, who offers to sprinkle corn grains around the school in exchange for **₦35,000** to "multiply" enrolment. Bepo refuses the ritual. Ten years later, a news broadcast reports that Ogo has murdered a client who paid **₦29 million** for a fertility ritual — the spiritualist sits atop a chair placed over a grave-sized pit, and his accomplices club the client to death. So Bepo wakes one morning to find the feeling he had about his predecessor confirmed — he **quietly packs his belongings and leaves Beesway**. The director apologises, saying that night was for a "special prayer for his late father, who gave the land". Bepo says not a word.

Also embedded: Bepo's own small school, **Fruitful Future**, founded after Youth Service, grew enrolment to over 70 by the third session using the pupil-teaches-parent tactic he picked up inside Nigeria at **Waari, Delta State**, during National Youth Service. The school later crumbled from road failure and a burst water main.

## Chapter 8 — Missions Unaccomplished (PDF 40–42; printed pages 41–43)

The prefect election is fast approaching. The system at Stardom works like this. **The price of nomination forms scales by post** — Head Boy and Head Girl candidates pay **₦50,000 each**; their deputies pay **₦40,000 each**; other posts, including Chapel Prefect, Health Prefect, Social Prefect, Labour Prefect and Punctuality Prefect, cost **₦25,000**. The aspirants face a five-member screening panel of teachers, non-academics and two alumni observers; candidates with a clear record and good grades sit an informal interview — each candidate speaks for **three minutes**. When names go up on the board, voters choose, and the old order is suspended because of a legal dispute.

The dispute: the school contests a racy Prefect's speech on Speech Day, where **Banky** (SS2, Social Prefect hope) attacks **Tosh** — also SS2 — calling him **"the son of an ex-convict"** in his speech. Tosh walks out of the hall; pundits rage; family honour explodes. The parents are two politicians of **the two leading parties** — progressive and liberal candidates from APC and PDP respectively. Banky's mother had beaten Tosh's mother for the PTA treasurer's seat. Their sons were finalists in the **Best Dancer** contest in JSS 3, a hip-hop final where judges crowned Banky on a 3–2 vote; Tosh's mother complained bitterly about the judging. The elder of the two homes — Tosh's mother and father, **Chief Didi Ogba**, a senior lawyer who served 36 months detention over a **₦2.5 billion** misappropriation allegation, acquitted and ordered his company to refund it, never directly guilty — demands published apologies; Banky's parents refuse. And in the background, Bepo worries more about what happens to the **Breath Project** — the massive invention-club initiative, headed by Bepo, developing a locally manufactured smartphone using recycled materials and funded externally with an NGO, **Life Grid**. The school will announce the phone, and Bepo promises to return from London to be part of the launch.

## Chapter 9 — Laughing Waterfalls (PDF 43–46; printed pages 44–47)

Stardom runs about three excursions per year — a domestic one every term and an international one every year, roughly 70% reliable. The destinations around the country include **Ikogosi Warm Springs (Ekiti)**, discovered in **1852 by Rev. John S. McGee** from his mission base at Igede-Ekiti, for a youth camp meant for the newly converted king's children — the myth was that he defied locals' warnings that the springs are inhabited by supernatural forces. **Erin-Ijesa (Olumirin) Waterfalls (Osun)** is said to have been discovered by a daughter of **Oduduwa** — or by "Akinla, the founder of Erin-Ijesa" — in **1140 AD**. **Owu Waterfalls in Kwara** is the highest in West Africa, about **120 metres** above water level, with a 330-foot escarpment. **Gurara Falls (Niger State)** was discovered by a Gwari hunter named **Buba** around 1748, nearly two centuries before Europeans arrived in 1925. Also on the list: Yankari Game Reserve in Bauchi, the National War Museum in Umuahia (Abia), the Hanging Lake in Ado Awaye (Oyo), the Kano Palace, the Ooni of Ife's palace, Obafemi Awolowo University at Ile-Ife — the alma mater of Mrs Ibidun Gloss, the proprietor — Sungbo's Eredo in Ijebu (an 800–1,000 AD defensive ditch), and Lagos's own sights (the National Theatre in Iganmu, Lagos Mall, Epe, and charity institutions as a reminder).

But the **Badagry** trip anchors the theme. Forty SSS 1–3 students, led by Bepo, visit the palace of **the Akran of Badagry** to remind the students where slavery happened — the first port of call is a tour of the palace; the monarch tells them that bad guys sold their own kin — the event of the late 1720s, when Popo refugees came to the coast. They visit the **First Storey Building (Henry Townsend, 1846)**, where Ajayi Crowther, the first African Anglican bishop, stayed and produced the first translation of the Yoruba Bible; and the **Anglican Nursery and Primary School (formerly the Nursery of the Infant Church, established 1843)**, which bore the first church school in Nigeria. They pass through the **Black Heritage Museum** — the trans-Atlantic slave museum at Badagry — and nothing touches them like the slave trade. Bepo, emotionally undone, realises that the same "japa" movement today is Africans voluntarily trading themselves for dollars and pounds to the former masters who looted them — chains on the economy, just not on the wrists. The visit ends at the **Point of No Return** — the jetty from which slaves were shipped.
## Chapter 10 — Passport Pains (PDF 47–52; printed pages 48–53)

Bepo is a procrastinator. For two years he lets his passport lapse. The COVID-19 pandemic makes renewal impossible, and post-pandemic tales of new rules, hassles and booklet non-availability spook him. By **the first quarter of 2022**, renewal is an emergency. Realising he cannot bear the maddening crowding at the Lagos office, he explores the Ibadan option. **A friend-of-a-friend connect in the Ibadan office — an unofficial agent called "Tai", a business-centre operator — arranges a direct line**: he charges **₦100,000 for a 10-year, 64-page passport, and a further ₦20,000 to expedite capture** — though the same service is promised by the government at a fraction of the cost. The agent hints that his prices reflect "the rules", not real corruption. Tuesday afternoon Bepo sets off; he decides not to travel in his car (the Lagos–Ibadan road is bad and security is shaky); instead he catches a shared Toyota Sienna — but everyone aboard appears destined for the world beyond. The expressway astonishes him: compared with the old days of agonising traffic on his Ile-Ife excursion, the road today is an open highway with fewer stalls — and almost nobody observes the toll-gate discipline.

**By 6 a.m. the next morning**, Bepo waits at the Agodi-Gate Passport Office in Ibadan, as the law demands. The route to the office is surprisingly orderly compared to Lagos — queues and procedure are civil. The agent acts as the process man — he collects Bepo's passport, verifies his NIN (National Identity Number), and pushes the booklet through for data capture within about three weeks, pointing out the hall full of people who have spent "forever" there. When the officials offer to expedite things, Bepo parts with an extra **₦2,000** as courtesy — learning that the waiting game can be unbearable.

## Chapter 11 — Point of No Return (PDF 53–57; printed pages 54–58)

The school organises a huge farewell for Bepo. The programme runs for three days:

- A **banner** stretches across the main hall — *"To our most committed principal…"* — revealing for the first time that "Bepo" is short for **Adebepo Adewale**, displayed publicly as "Adebepo Adewale".
- Bepo shifts the Emirates flight by a week; the airline charges him **$100** as a change-of-date fee, and the school pays it.
- On **Wednesday** there is a novelty match — students versus staff. The referee (who later turns out to be **Audu**) repeatedly rejects goals, **awards penalties five times**, and the game ends in a stalemate; the goalless brim stays good for the kids.
- On **Friday** there is a debate on whether Nigeria gains most from its artistic and cultural output or from its science and technology; the arts team wins, citing film, music and culture as Nigeria's strongest international exports; Bepo listens with admiration, watching his seed pay off.
- The cultural programme includes a skit imitating **Bepo** at assembly (his voice, palms in the pockets, the "PrinciPL" catchphrase, "PrinciPL, not Principè"), to excitement and laughter.
- **Bata**, **Atilogwu**, **Koroso** and the **Canoe dance** (revived from the October show he took the students to see at National Theatre) — the evening confronts Bepo with a spectacle of Nigerian culture. Then the Canoe dance lands: **deep in an emotional trance, he cries out "Nooo!"** during it, recalling Badagry. He collects himself, apologises jokingly, and the audience applauds anyway.
- **The MD rises** and gives him the largest leave fee in Stardom's history — a flat envelope. The crowd expects something absurd; it turns out to be **at least $10,000** in a domiciliary cheque ("the highest Stardom has ever presented any disengaging member of staff"). She recounts that her father, the late Chief David Aje, hired him after he had **applied and not shown up at the interview — having only had a chat** — and waited eight days for him to come, earning his trust on the spot. She praises his pillar-qualities — **skill, drive, empathy and morals** — and calls him a **blessing to Stardom**. Bepo weeps at the podium.

## Chapter 12 — Dawn (PDF 58–64, plus the back cover; printed pages 59–64)

Bepo's flight is headed for London, planned for **10:00 p.m. on a Saturday**; check-in is at **5:30 p.m.**; an ominously sober silence hangs over the halls. The **VP Apeh** drives him, with **Oyelana** and **Audu**, to the airport; the two vehicles are escorted through Ikeja underflow; traffic is normal. At departures; his **seat is 56** (window-side), **Gate 2A**. At check-in a female official checks his ticket and smiles; alarms; his phone is in his pocket — he can't find her call to convey that he's on the way. He boards, and the plane takes off at **10:45 p.m.** Some time later — he is groggy — he is violently jolted awake by a dream in which a white man stands over him **screaming "Enter!"** in a slave camp; **Bepo cries out "No!"** — leaping out of his seat. The airline officials are startled and curious; he is gently calmed down; the plane flies London-bound.

**Monday at Stardom is jarring.** The whole school turns down. He isn't there. The classroom absentee trend dramatically warns the students that something is wrong. Apeh visits the assembly, and her flat-toned welcome fails to replicate Bepo's. At the gate, staff are shocked — **SHOUTS echo that he is back: "I'm back! I'm back! I didn't go!"** — and the school erupts with him returning, with tears, dancing and singing **Stardom's school anthem**:

*"We're Stars, we're Stars! We're Stars of Stardom! We're Stars and we have won! No night so dark! No cloud so mean! Yet Stardom Schools will win!"*

His mission is clear in his own words: he didn't go; this is where his heart is.
## Characters — what JAMB asks about each of them

**Bepo Adewale (Adebepo)** is the principal of Stardom Schools, with about 24 years in post. He was head at the nursery and primary arm for 4 years. He trained as a teacher after studying English and History at the University of Benin. He grew up poor — the ₦2,500 he "ate" while jobless still burns him. He is a teacher of high moral conviction, deeply attached to his learners; he is moved by example (Akindele-style japa tales, and parents' gratitude); he tears easily; he is light-complexioned; he stands six feet two inches; his voice is strong; he keeps a left-pocket hand habit; he is always fixing other people's problems — the embodiment of what a school needs.

**Seri** is Bepo's wife — a nurse in London who emigrated with the children. She is the heartbeat of the "japa" argument — loving, practical, not sentimental about Lagos. She repeatedly phones him about the passport, the house clearing, and the food items to bring; her health-worker status and the family's suffering pull Bepo across.

**Nike and Kike** are their two children, in Britain. Nike is at university; Kike is in secondary school; both benefited from fee-free public schooling — a key proof-point of the japa case.

**Mrs Ibidun Gloss** is the proprietor and MD of Stardom Schools, a second-generation operator whose father was **Chief David Aje**. The Vice-Principal and Vice-Proprietor is Grace Apeh. Gloss describes herself as a reluctant operator — notably sharp, humour-filled and commercially pivotal; she has a bad back (the chair-problem); she drives major decisions (salary raises, the ₦250,000 cap, the F9 sack policy) with sharp commercial sense.

**Mrs Grace Apeh, the VP**, is the steady administrator who runs the school when Bepo breaks down — organised, powerful, gentle-hearted — the one who truly keeps Stardom stable. She consults him, motivates staff, and is trusted with the arrangements of his travel and farewell.

**Mr Audu**, fine arts, is the joker of staff meetings — witty, musical by heart, the school's spirit at assembly, a top dancer. He performs, motivates and defuses (the "Pardoned" verdict in the grammar debate), and he is among the few to directly confront Bepo about his plan of leaving.

**Mr Justus Anabel** is the chemistry teacher — he is the man Apeh asks to close the assembly when Bepo cracks.

**Mr Oye Wande**, the physics teacher and pastor, is quiet and moral — a friend to Bepo. The MD asks him to sit with Bepo when he broke down; he is among the first to recommend the school let Bepo go home for a break; he prayed with Bepo later.

**Mr Oyelana**, the CRK teacher, is the comparative-religion teacher and acting deputy in assemblies — dry humour; the one who chortles about the fact that Bepo's salary dwarfs everyone else's dreams.

**Fafore** is the Nigerian archetype of the struggling teacher — 22 years of service, an Ifo (Ogun) home, poor pay, a 4 a.m. commute, two children and an expected third; almost dismissed for one grammatically correct sentence.

**Mrs Ignatius** is the hysterical parent in the late-night phone call about the visa case — her oil-firm manager husband left her; their youngest girl, Favour, is not his DNA match; the family plan collapsed publicly.

**Mr & Mrs Mary Ladele** are the panicked parents who attempted to pull three siblings out of Stardom after one girl's nightmares about a teacher with tribal marks.

**Chief Didi Ogba** is the fierce parent and politician whose son's Dancer saga almost ended Stardom's election — a top lawyer who himself served time (a ₦2.5 billion contract misappropriation; the company ordered to refund it; 36 months detention; a five-year trial). His son Tosh was born of a senator and had his career haunted by it.

**Akindele, Riike, Sola, Hope, Jare and Chief Waliem** are all the characters Bepo hears about who represent the highs and lows of japa — Akindele (55, working 12-hour days in freight or corrections), Riike (built houses in Ibadan within three years), Sola (Manchester, free schooling, an asthma and seizure-care triumph), Hope (whose wife stops financing him), Jare (banker-to-old-people-carer), and Chief Waliem (once a rich man bankrolled social events, banks seized everything).
## Places and organisations worth knowing

**Stardom Schools (A-List School), Lekki** is the elite school — hyper-competitive WASSCE culture, every student council election conducted openly, every prefect post paying a premium, and cheating destroyed. **Stardom Kiddies** is its nursery and primary arm (where Bepo headed for four years and earned the nickname). **Stardom Hub** is the property wing to which Mr Ayesoro is transferred.

**The Stardom Staff Cooperative Society** is self-managed and accountable to itself — no juju-boss, no outside pressure. The purse holds about **₦95 million**, most staff are borrowers, loans are now capped at **₦250,000** per member, officials are elected, and every loan needs the Proprietor's signature.

**The Ikoyi / Agodi Gate passport office in Ibadan** is where Bepo tries to get his renewal done quickly — queue system is rigorous, and an agent industry (Tai) flourishes in the gaps.

**The National Theatre, Iganmu** is the destination of the cultural excursion — host of the Song of the Lagoon.

**Bridging organisations** — national media and radio, MCAN and others — are all queried by Stardom's trips.

**Farms, tourist sites and waterfalls** — Ikogosi (1852, found by Rev. John S. McGee), Owu in Kwara (the highest West African waterfall, about 120 metres tall / 330 feet), Gurara, Erin-Ijesa / Olumirin, Yankari — fill the excursion calendar.

**Libraries, museums and relic locations** — the Black Heritage Museum at Badagry documents the 1500–1900 trans-Atlantic slave trade.

## Processes you should be able to narrate

**How a prefect becomes a prefect:** a student contests by buying a nomination form, pays the specified fee, faces a screening panel, writes a three-minute speech, names go up on the board, others vote, and the MD decides — while a school-house (Banky / Tosh) rivalry openly persists.

**How a teacher is sacked:** a complaint comes in, the MD summons the teacher to her office, the principal reports on the matter, the teacher steps down (and usually pleads), a sack letter goes out, and the teacher cries — or protests.

**How a passport is renewed (as Bepo did it):** it expires, COVID-19 delays it, family pressure forces the renewal, an agent is contacted at Agodi Gate (Ibadan), **₦100,000 + ₦20,000 + a possible ₦2,000 tip**, the NIN is validated, the photograph is captured, and the booklet is collected within about three weeks.

**The physical movement from arrival to departure:** the children are handed off, the bags are done, the check-in happens, security passes, the boarding gate opens, and then the plane takes off at 10:45 p.m.

**Assembly, dismissal and the school's music** — the crafting of a student community in Nigeria's South West.
## Themes and techniques you should know — themes cheat sheet

**Decision-making vs. loyalty** — this novel is one long meditation on choosing home over money, and service over self-interest.

**Failed emigration** — the japa dream is examined with nuance: for many it works, for some it is dust.

**Tradition vs. modernity** — Yoruba proverbs are woven throughout; slang like "japa" and "waka" echoes across the text.

**The power of a teacher** — Stardom stands or falls on its staff. Bepo is the proof of "the master carver who gives his craft to the next generation"; the shrines of the play are the dusty libraries, classrooms, assemblies and examinations.

## Techniques cheat sheet

**Foreshadowing** — the lost telephone call; the prayer with the children.

**Catharsis** — the tears at both ends of the novel — the opening breakdown and the closing weeping.

### 2.3 Chapter 3 — Migration Tales — PDF 13–17

**The pay formats** — Talks from Canada, Britain and the United States show hourly, daily, weekly and monthly pay against Nigeria's monthly salary — hourly gives shift freedom.

**The proverb link** — The Yoruba saying is that the worker is paid whether the boss gains or not — the wage system seen as a growth ideal.

**The US sums** — A friend says $150 to $250 daily, depending on job and town, four days weekly to $2,400 monthly; dreamers multiply by ₦1,600 for big naira.

**The debt flight** — Some sell goods that aren't theirs; some take loans and flee; some repay from abroad; some never look back.
