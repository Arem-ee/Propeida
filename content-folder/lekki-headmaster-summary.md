# The Lekki Headmaster — the complete revision

You're sitting Section A of Use of English, and ten questions come from one book. This sheet is the whole novel in our own words, with nothing close to the actual text — read it twice and Section A holds no surprises.

Kabir Alabi Garba's The Lekki Headmaster was published in 2023 by Winepress (2024 copyright; BASMALLAH COMMS special edition; ISBN 978-978-60211-2-6). It tells the story of **Adebepo Adewale, called Bepo** — the long-serving principal of Stardom Schools in Lekki, Lagos, who climbs slowly from doubting his own exit to choosing his school over emigration. This sheet is written to stand alone — plot, names, numbers, places, processes, themes and exam traps. Learn it properly and you need no other summary; read the book itself when you can, but you can sit Section A without a single blank.

**The shape of the whole story** — Stardom is an elite private school in Lekki whose routine runs on assembly, examinations, staff meetings, excursions, cultural programmes and discipline. Wife Seri (a nurse in London), Nike and Kike (their children, settled in England) press Bepo to emigrate; he is torn because he loves his learners. The twelve chapters run Dusk → Dawn as a day-night circle — opening breakdown, the midpoint dread, the passport pains, the farewell, the flight, the return.

## Chapter 1 — Dusk… (PDF 4–8; printed pages 5–9)

A routine morning assembly is thrown off-course. Bepo walks to the podium from the back, moving slowly, smiling little, and takes the microphone from **Angel, the chapel prefect** — at the very moment the *pep talk* (launched seven years earlier) has everyone excited. The student speaker is **Ikenna Egbu**, an SSS science poet from a broadcaster-family, back from a Jos (Plateau State) excursion, describing Lamingo Dam, the University of Jos, Shere Hills, Wase Rocks and Solomon Lar **Amusement Park** — and praising Jos over London or New York, closing by declaring he would rather settle in Jos than japa to Canada or the UK. His mother is an award-winning poet (a broadcaster); Ikenna mixes results with verse.

Then Bepo freezes. He tries to speak; nothing comes. His head drops to his shoulders; his eyes drop to the tiles; he cries — first trickles, then a full breakdown — and drops the microphone, which clatters off the twin speakers. **Vice-Principal Grace Apeh** and four staff rush to him; **Mrs Titi (the nurse)** brings a handkerchief; **Mr Justus Anabel (chemistry teacher)** is told to dissolve the assembly; Apeh and nurse and **Mr Oyelana (CRK)** walk him to his office, face buried in palms. He lies there softly for over 30 minutes; more teachers arrive; the **guidance counsellor, Mrs Beke Egbin**, joins the small group; the pleading partner in the dark office moves into **the MD's office, second floor of the Admin Building** — proprietor **Mrs Ibidun Gloss**, "popularly called MD", who drives back from morning business, takes off her glasses, drops them on a tea stool, and receives him. He still will not say why. The MD calls in **Mr Oye Wande (physics teacher and pastor)**; he sits beside Bepo, reminds him of his cardinal position, and hears nothing; Bepo writes his wife's international number on the back of a card he takes from his grey suit pocket; two calls do not ring. She sends him home, thinking the school is a place for learning and earning, not a rehabilitation centre — and that parents pressing would follow.

Context the examiner can use:
- **Boarding fee cuts**: from ₦250,000 to ₦165,000 per session — above 80% of parents moved their children in, punctuality jumped (almost everyone at 7:45 a.m.); school then quietly raised **Excursion and Other Items** by ₦93,000. **Mr Audu (fine arts)** — the staffroom's biting humour — quips the MD is "a witch and a wizard rolled into one".
- Assembly runs the **second stanza of the National Anthem as prayer on Tuesdays and Thursdays** ("Oh God of creation, direct our noble cause…") and Christian/Muslim prayers plus the first stanza on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, then the school anthem
- The previous day: Stardom had celebrated **above 90% WASSCE success**, snacks, wine and ₦20,000 to all SSS 3 teachers; ₦30,000 to teachers of distinction-scoring subjects. Only two teachers had two-candidates-with-Ds lines to answer to: **Mr Obong Ukaku (Chemistry) and Miss Taye Kareem (Geography)** — but no candidate scored F9, so no dismissal (Stardom's rule: an F9 gets the teacher sacked; the previous year even the MD's cousin **Mr Funso Daniels** was not spared).
- The staff tease Bepo that he answers every quarrel with the same settled manner, the way the old TV drama "Village Headmaster"'s **King Oloja** resolved matters; he mimics the character too well and the nickname sticks — **"The Lekki Headmaster"** — even after he becomes principal. He also always corrects "Principo" pronunciation: **"it is 'PrinciPL,' not 'Principo.'"**

## Chapter 2 — The Enticement (PDF 9–12; printed pages 10–13)

After about five days of what feels like interrogation, Bepo admits it: he plans to **leave Nigeria for the United Kingdom**. His marriage was at the brink because he dragged his feet; Seri, the nurse, is in London with **Nike** (now at university) and **Kike** (still at secondary school); the teaching post in the UK is secured. He loves Stardom and his learners, feels it is wrong to abandon a developing nation for an already-developed one — but family pressure is stronger.

The background economics — drawn for you:
- Stardom pays Bepo about **₦400,000/month**; the UK post promises about **£3,600** per month's entitlements; at about **₦1,700 per pound** the prospective income reads as about **₦6 million**. **Mr Oyelana (CRK)** notes Bepo will spend in pounds, not naira, weakening the naira-remittance argument; **Audu** shoves that away: if he sends £1,000 home a month, that is above ₦1.7 million — nobody making ₦400,000 can match that. Audu mocks him as "bewitched", thrusts books under an armpit, announces he is "heading to the airport" — and is late for class, as usual. His jokes save him.
- Bepo had been planning to **retire at 55** — four years away; he had been attending entrepreneurship seminars, picturing himself founding **a school of his own** after Stardom (masala of experience plus endless ambition) or failing that, **transport leasing**, and smaller ventures: farm, food, sachet pure water. The cautionary tale of his own failure is always in his mouth: **Fruitful Future School**, the small neighbourhood school he and a colleague started shortly after National Youth Service, which **collapsed due to socio-economic challenges in the location**. Seri would never finance any project holding him down in Nigeria.

Something else happened first: years earlier, Bepo was pinched out of a previous job and went without any salary. At "Tyana Ipaja" (as the OCR page 27-28 reads — a crowded Agbeni-style face-me-I-face-you house in Iyana Ipaja, Lagos), at the height of financial drought, he **"ate" ₦2,500 of tenants' electricity tariff money he had collected** and lied about remitting it; when NEPA eventually cut the cable, the furious tenants discovered the truth, mocked him as "Oga Tisa" (Teacher Oga), and **Iya Mathew**, fierce wife of co-tenant Mr Adio, leapt onto him with a bowl of **elubo (cassava powder)** dumped full over his head while singing "Oga Tisa, ole! O na owo ina!" — "Mr Teacher, thief! He spent the electricity tariff!" That shame is the cruelest moment Bepo carries; it explains his intolerance for waiting jobless.

## Chapter 3 — Migration Tales (PDF 13–17; printed pages 14–18)

Once Bepo has made up his mind, he floods himself with diaspora stories — and this chapter is where JAMB plucks its most frequent factoids. Key claims, told in Bepo's voice as he listens to tales from abroad:

- **Hourly pay works abroad**. A friend in the US says one can earn **between $150 and $250 a day** for up to four days a week, i.e. up to about **$2,400 a month** — usually by the route of japa dreams. The Yoruba proverb at issue: *"oga ta, oga o ta, owo alaaru a pe"* — whether an employer gains or loses, the worker still takes home his full pay. That is called the *pragmatic wage* system and is held up as one of the ideals powering developed countries.
- People ignore the down-side: rent, taxes and maintenance are paid in hard currencies; what remains still transfers as good sums home (the Nigerian who switches from driving to a maintenance job earning **$500 a day** works out to about **₦800,000 daily**; cabbies outearn toilet-cleaners)
- **The government admits about 3,000 doctors emigrate yearly**; mass emigration includes many graduates and nurses, especially those **pursuing Master's degrees**; study visas allow holder-plus-spouse-plus-children, so families sell *all* their assets (houses, land, cars, office/home goods); some sold what wasn't theirs, some fled before loan repayment, some repay slowly from abroad, some never look back. Stardom gossip from Bepo's own administration: **Mr Nku**, who took a ₦2 million cooperative loan and vanished in two days into the "japa wind"; and **a school driver** who tried to steal a school bus to fund his son's college education abroad and confessed to the police.
- **Sola** — former Home Economics teacher at Stardom, dismissed to a three-month suspension for setting up a creche near the school (Bepo led the investigation that showed it was casual labour, saved her from sacking, she kept pay entitlements after monitoring) — emigrated to the UK six months before from London with her husband; she now works the British system and calls to brief Bepo. They married rent instead of fees — they sold everything and still borrowed about **₦4 million** — and live (and studied) — husband is currently on a Master's supported by her earnings. She earns up to **£200 a day**. Manchester is their base because London rents of £1,000–£1,500 are far beyond their means — not furnished, **£650 per month**; they pay month-by-month, not a year ahead; rail from Manchester to London is **about 30 minutes**; children there get **free primary and secondary schooling** (Nike and Kike were also beneficiaries of UK free schooling before Nike began to pay university fees; wife paid the bulk); there are education loans and scholarships for poor homes; a three-year-old who had a seizure drew **two ambulances plus a car in under five minutes**, birthday in hospital, gifts showered by staff.
- **Jare** is a young man who left a banking job and wept to find caring for an old couple in London docked his dignity; **Hope**, an accountant, applied to a UK Master's supported by his wife; barely four months after landing she simply stopped paying because "the demands of her job are too heavy" — the wife's own parents in Nigeria agreed with her verdict
- **Riike** — a woman now in the US — in under three years bought **two houses in Ibadan**; she has three children in fee-free primary/secondary foundation schools (till 2027, when the first will reach university); she's brought two sisters, and hopes to bring her brother **Wemimo** (the youngest) once visa hurdles clear
- **Akindele** — a man who migrated to the US (invited by a US citizen he met at a Lagos media company) at 55, after an 18-year marriage ended in divorce (three children between 16 and 10); he now lives with his new woman; his first job is freight loading, followed by **correctional service**; he notes he can't yet invite any family because he needs time and financial base; he says flat out: "there is no easy job in America" — even Nigerian retired permanent secretaries end up as casual workers moving mail at 70 years

## Chapter 4 — A Case of Visa Denied (PDF 18–22; printed pages 19–23)

**Mrs Ignatius** — a parent at Stardom — calls Bepo at his room at **Adeniyi Jones, Ikeja**, at **11:55pm**; she sobs that her son **Ibe** is not home yet; she is one of those parents who overstretch his cordiality by discussing their marital life. During the call Bepo learns:
- Her oil-firm-manager husband (Ibe) had bought into japa — they had been planning to relocate to London; he complained he was sick of the system and vowed no child should be left behind; the economic maths drew them ($1,000 remittance/month × ₦1,600 black-market rate = **₦1,600,000** monthly — an amount dwarfed by even a senior lawyer's salary of ₦15,000 a month)
- Mrs Ignatius had learned tailoring and hairdressing, including **irun didi**, and the bridal art — she pulled a start as a clerical officer and went for specific **Yoruba braids** (suku, kojusoko, onilegogoro) with the promise of **$100 per head** in the UK (equal to about ₦160,000). Her husband had wound down his clearing-and-forwarding side business in preparation.
- At the advanced visa stage, an **DNA test showed Ibe is not the father of their eldest daughter, Favour (15, SS2 girl at Stardom)** — the family never survived the fallout. The visa was refused; the wife tells Bepo: Ibe refuses to believe it; he says "Favour and I have to go" — Favour does not know the saga; her brother **Lyi** suspects why the visas were refused (visa rules require paternity evidence for applicants below 18); Ibe left home two hours before Bepo finishes the call; Bepo thinks immediately of a *memo* on school events as a possible book — a recurring motif.

A parallel thread in the same chapter: **Mrs Mary Ladele**, watching a Nollywood romance film at home at 1 p.m. in GRA (her husband Dele, senior civil servant in Abuja) — sleeping children, a mild rule of bed at 10 p.m., an enemy named **Mr Ayesoro**, a **Government teacher with deep tribal marks** (nicknamed **Mr Wala / Mr Owata** by pupils for his cuts) who meets three-month-old **Bibi** at a Saturday basketball practice; the child is terrified; she dreams of him repeatedly; her relations eventually force a house transfer to **Green House**; the Stardom management, anxious about losing the Ladele kids to a rival school, **transfers Ayesoro to Stardom Hub, the property arm of Stardom Group of Companies** — diverted from teaching altogether.
